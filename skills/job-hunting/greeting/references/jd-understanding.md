# JD understanding: from text to the employer's need

Do not choose a template from a preset taxonomy or a keyword dictionary. Work out from **this JD's actual content** what the employer is trying to solve.

## 1. Extract the real work

Keep a short internal note:

1. **End problem**: once hired, what business result, process, user experience or organisational outcome must this person change?
2. **Scope of responsibility**: what must they do themselves, whom must they coordinate, and what level of judgement or delivery do they own?
3. **Success criteria**: how will the employer judge good work — quality, speed, revenue, experience, user outcomes, compliance? Keep only what the JD states or clearly implies. Do not invent KPIs.
4. **Key abilities**: what substantive abilities does the work require? Separate must-have, preferred and bonus.
5. **Hard conditions and risks**: degree, location, licences, specific industry experience, years of experience. Treat these as fit risks for the host or user to weigh. Do not put them, or the candidate's gaps against them, into the message.

## 2. Find the core need; don't mechanically cover everything

- Trust concrete tasks and success criteria over the title, slogans or keyword density.
- When the JD lists many duties, find the **one problem that runs through them**. One message does not have to answer every bullet.
- You may infer *why* the employer needs an ability, but keep the inference separate from what the JD states.
- When a role mixes directions, don't force a single profile. Compare how much weight each responsibility carries, and keep the uncertainty.
- Similar or near-duplicate JDs still deserve a fresh angle based on where their duties differ. Never copy the previous result.

## 3. Write a need statement for evidence search

Internally, form a short `need statement`:

- the work that must get done (verb and object)
- the important constraint or ability involved
- the result the employer would recognise as success

You may mark one to three short verbatim fragments of the JD as anchors, but the message has **no obligation to quote the JD**. Any quoted anchor must be continuous text that is actually visible; a paraphrase must never be presented as a quote.

## 4. Failure signs

- Every job gets the same self-description and opening.
- Only nouns are recognised; the result the role owns is missing.
- Requirements in the JD are written up as the candidate's experience.
- Clients, organisation or internal processes are inferred before the JD has been read in full.
- Target-domain terms are forced onto the candidate's past work, creating the impression they have already done the target job.
- The candidate's *source*-domain details (their old industry's jargon, product names, client types) dominate a message to an unrelated industry, so the reader concludes "wrong background" before seeing the transferable capability.
