# Message composition: natural, coherent, targeted

The goal is the message the candidate would actually send to the recipient, not a report analysing the job.

## 1. Decide the value first

Before writing, settle one thing: after reading, what true experience or ability of the candidate should the reader believe will be useful in this job?

- **Natural self-introduction**: introduce the candidate in a way that is useful for this conversation and backed by facts. Do not require a job title, years, credentials, school or past employer. Do not open with a label from an unrelated field (for example, "I'm a fintech PM" to a search-product team): it tells the reader "not one of us" before they reach the evidence.
- **A real bridge**: state how the employer's core problem relates to the candidate's experience. The link can be continuity of the problem, transfer of a method, or relevance of a result. Phrases like "I've also done this" or "I meet your requirements" are not a bridge.
- **Action and result**: give actions, outputs or meaningful outcomes that let the reader sense depth. Mechanism alone or result alone is fine, depending on the goal.
- **A light close**: whether and how to invite a reply depends on the channel and the user's preferences. No mandatory question or sign-off.

These are quality goals, **not a fixed four-part structure**. Choose order, phrasing, tone and emphasis afresh each time.

## 2. One line of argument

A message needs a clear line of cause and effect, or of task. Every sentence should answer "why does this follow the previous sentence, and why does this reader need it?"

Common ways messages fall apart:

| Pattern | What it looks like | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Collage | "You need X and also Y; previously I led Z and W." The two halves never connect | Choose one need, one experience, and say how they relate |
| Restating the JD | Repeating two or more of the JD's requirements back to the reader | Respond to the need instead of echoing it |
| Bolt-on | "…; I've also done Q." A second, unrelated achievement appended at the end | Delete it, or show its real link to the main line |
| Product switch | The first half is about one project, the second about another, with no stated relation | Stay in one context, or explain the link ("the same approach also…") |
| Quote stacking | Several quoted fragments of the JD | Quote at most one, or none |
| Opening with a lecture | "The hardest part of AI safety is…" | Address the recipient's task, not the industry |

**Deletion test.** Read each clause and ask: if this were removed, would the reader still understand the message and its link to the job? If yes, remove it. Internal mechanisms ("first it classifies intent, then routes to…"), self-commentary ("this is exactly my habit"), process narration and second achievements are the usual candidates. Keep a mechanism only when the JD's need *is* that mechanism.

When revising, decide which judgement or line is changing before touching words. Synonym swaps are not a rewrite.

## 3. Speak to the recipient, not about them

Don't treat the recipient as a bystander. Avoid meta-analysis such as "this role", "this JD", "from a fit perspective" or "the profile for this position". You can refer to tasks they actually listed, but don't explain their hiring logic back to them or quote the JD at length.

Avoid empty self-praise, ingratiating declarations, abstract skill lists and lectures about the industry. Let facts, decisions and results show the value. Don't feel bound to mention a particular tool, credential, major or achievement every time.

## 4. Language and depth

- Match difficulty to the recipient (known recruiter, hiring manager, or unknown), the JD's density of terminology and the user's preferences.
- Use a technical term when it helps prove relevance and the JD uses it too. If the recipient may not understand it, use plain business language without losing the fact.
- **Translate the candidate's world into the recipient's.** Internal product names, codenames, team slang and source-industry specifics often mean nothing to an outsider. Describe the work by what it does ("a system where several agents draft and cross-check long reports"), not by its internal name or the old industry's vocabulary, unless that vocabulary is itself relevant (for example, the same industry). Translating vocabulary must never change the facts or imply target-industry experience.
- Don't strip every trace of the candidate's history, and don't invent same-named experience just because the JD uses a term.
- Length follows the platform, the user's preferences and the information density. Without explicit limits, aim for one screen with nothing redundant.
- Use only forms of address you actually know. Don't infer the recipient's name, gender, age or internal company information.

## 5. Keep plans and past results apart

The candidate's views on future product directions, methods or target markets can be expressed as ideas or interests. They must not read as launched, validated or customer-proven past results. Past work keeps its real scope and outcome.

## 6. Final read-aloud test

- Is this something the candidate would send, or the AI explaining its writing choices?
- Does every sentence serve the same core need?
- Is it restating the JD instead of responding to it?
- Is there a mechanism, a technical term or a second project that could go?
- Does it hint at over-promising, false proficiency or delivery that never happened?
- Would a reader from the target industry understand every noun?
