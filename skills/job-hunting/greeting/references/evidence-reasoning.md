# Evidence reasoning: dynamic selection and fidelity to facts

**There is no job-category-to-evidence mapping, no fixed fact cards, no product list, no ranking of achievements and no default endorsement.** Start only from the candidate facts the host supplied for this request.

## 1. Read each piece of experience as an action that can be proven

Look at each candidate fact from these angles. They are not required database fields.

- the real problem at the time, who it served and under what constraints
- what the candidate actually did, what they had authority to decide, and where collaboration began and ended
- the methods, tools or ways of working used, and how proficient the candidate really was
- verifiable outputs, effects, and who the results belong to
- source, date, corrections and how any metric was defined

A word appearing often does not make that experience the most relevant.

## 2. Judge relevance dynamically

Compare **problem similarity, task similarity, transferable capability, strength of evidence and relevance of the result**:

- **Direct evidence**: the candidate did work closely matching the target role's problem. State it plainly, within its original scope.
- **Transferable evidence**: the domain may differ, but the judgement, mechanism, delivery standard or success criteria are explainably similar. Describe the completed work, then why it matters for this task. Never imply the target-scenario work was already done.
- **Indirect evidence or background**: adds credibility, but usually doesn't deserve the main spot in a short message.
- **Unsupported**: the JD needs an ability the facts don't show. Do not paper over it with wording; record it as an internal gap.

Highly relevant but weak evidence loses to slightly less relevant but solid evidence. If both are weak, say so (see `low_fit` in [host-contract.md](host-contract.md)).

## 3. Choosing the main line

Choose the **most distinctive, most provable fact** that answers the employer's core need as the main line. To show real cause and effect you may bring in an earlier experience's prerequisite skill or result, but do not stitch projects together without reason.

There is no fixed number of experiences, facts or figures. The reader just needs to understand, in limited space, what the candidate did and why it matters here. If a fact can be removed without weakening the argument, remove it.

**Low-bar proof.** Some accomplishments are common in the target field: anyone applying will claim them. Leading with one as the proof of an advanced ability weakens the message. Prefer evidence that is hard or distinctive for *this* need. If no single fact is strong enough, show a consistent, provable pattern instead: the same kind of ownership across several pieces of work. Don't let one isolated, easily discounted claim carry the message.

## 4. Preserve metrics, credentials and outcomes

Keep strictly:

- a number's original denominator, definition, scope, time window, sample, and offline or online status
- the line between the candidate's own contribution and the team's or company's results
- the difference between owned, led, contributed, assisted, proposed, designed, validated, launched and scaled
- the stage difference between research, understanding the theory, building a prototype and delivering a product
- business scale, user reach, revenue, profit, retention, deals and awards are not interchangeable

**Numbers are optional.** Use one only when its definition can be stated in a few words and it is the kind of measure the recipient cares about. A striking number whose meaning the reader cannot interpret reads as noise, or as inflation. A message with no numbers is often stronger.

## 5. Counterfactual check

For the most attractive claim in the draft, ask:

- If the recipient asks "which step was yours, and how do you know it worked?", is there a fact to answer with?
- If the project and industry names are removed, is there still an explainable capability link? If not, it may be keyword matching.
- Does the sentence imply the candidate has worked in the target scenario when they haven't?
- Would this evidence be just as relevant to a different job? If so, the message probably isn't targeted yet.

It is fine to choose no numbers, no credentials and no company names. That choice depends on what helps this message and on the host's constraints.
